Peak Power · Method note

How the model reads climate, and how it fits the site decision

For a data center investor screening a 75 MW campus. Based on the Data Center Climate Explorer as built on 5 Nov 2026.

The short version. For any point the model downloads three years of hourly temperature and humidity, turns them into five plain cooling measures, and grades the result Mild, Moderate or Demanding.

Important: that climate grade is shown next to the Prototype's overall site grade. It is not an input to it, and it does not change the grid-delay cost and carbon figures. It tells you how hard the site is to cool, and you weigh it yourself. Section 4 explains how.

1. From weather to a climate grade

1 · FetchThree full calendar years (2023–2025) of hourly temperature and relative humidity for the clicked point. ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo, ~10 km grid.
2 · Wet-bulbEach hour's wet-bulb temperature is calculated from temperature and humidity (Stull 2011).
3 · MeasureFive measures per year: degree days, 95th-percentile temperature, hot hours, humid hours, free-cooling share.
4 · GradeTwo simple rules turn the measures into Mild, Moderate or Demanding.
5 · ShowThe grade appears as a chip in the site brief, with its reasons and thresholds.

The five measures

MeasureHow it is calculatedWhy an investor cares
Cooling degree days (base 65 °F)For each day: mean temperature minus 65 °F, floored at zero. Summed over the year and averaged across the three years.A single number for the total cooling burden. More degree days means more cooling energy and a higher power bill.
95th-percentile temperatureThe temperature that 95% of hours fall below (sorted hourly values).The "typical hot day" the cooling plant must handle, without being skewed by one freak hour.
Hot hours (≥ 95 °F)Share of valid hours at or above 95 °F, times 8,760.Hours that stress air-cooled chillers and reduce their capacity.
Humid hours (wet-bulb ≥ 75 °F)Share of valid hours with calculated wet-bulb at or above 75 °F, times 8,760.Wet-bulb sets how far evaporative cooling can chill water. Many humid hours mean less free capacity and more mechanical help.
Free-cooling share (< 65 °F)Share of hours with outside air below 65 °F.Hours when outside air does most of the cooling, so the chillers can rest.
Wet-bulb (Stull 2011), T in °C, RH in % (RH limited to 5–99%): Tw = T·atan[0.151977·√(RH + 8.313659)] + atan(T + RH) − atan(RH − 1.676331) + 0.00391838·RH^1.5·atan(0.023101·RH) − 4.686035 Check: 68 °F (20 °C) at 50% humidity → 13.7 °C = 56.7 °F

"Hours a year" is the share of valid hours times 8,760, so a partly missing year does not understate it. Hours with missing readings are skipped, not guessed.

The grading rule

GradeRule
MildAll three must hold: at least 55% of hours below 65 °F, and at most 300 humid hours a year, and at most 100 hot hours a year.
DemandingAny one is enough: under 35% of hours below 65 °F, or more than 1,500 humid hours, or more than 1,000 hot hours.
ModerateEverything else. The brief names which measure held it back.

These thresholds are a team screening assumption, set in CLIMATE_RULES in js/climate-weather.js. They are shown in the panel so a judge or investor can challenge them. They were tuned against real data for five cities (next section), not taken from an engineering standard.

3. What the climate grade does not do

It is not in the overall grade

The Prototype's overall site grade is a weighted average of other factors. Climate is not among them:

  • Texas counties: Location 1 · Energy 2 · Policy 1.5 · Fiber 1 · Water 1 · Hazard 1 · Time to power 1
  • Other states: Location 1 · Time to power 2 (stands in for energy) · Policy 1.5 · Fiber 1 · Water 1 · Hazard 1

Excellent = no Bad factor and a nearly all Good average. Bad = energy is Bad or the average is mostly Bad. Water is graded separately by the Prototype from WRI Aqueduct data.

It does not change the plan numbers

The cost and carbon figures in "What this means for a 75 MW plan" come from the Prototype's engine for the Texas (ERCOT) packet case, with the grid connection late by 0, 6 or 12 months. They use packet prices, wind and solar profiles, not the local temperature.

The Prototype's separate "weather year" setting scales wind and solar profiles. It is unrelated to this climate summary.

Why keep them apart? Climate affects cooling energy and equipment, which the Prototype's dispatch model does not simulate. Folding an unmodelled factor into a grade judges can see through would make the overall grade look more precise than the evidence supports. Showing it beside the grade keeps each number honest.

4. How it affects the overall decision

Read the site brief top to bottom. Climate changes the decision through cost, equipment and risk, not through the grade arithmetic.

If the climate is…What it usually means for a 75 MW campusWhat to do with it
MildOutside air covers more than half the year. Lower cooling energy, lower operating cost, more headroom on hot days.Treat as a tiebreaker in favour of the site when grades are close. Still confirm the design day.
ModerateA real mechanical cooling plant is needed for a large part of the year. Cost and capacity sit in the middle.Look at which measure held it back (humid or hot hours) and ask the cooling engineer to size for it.
DemandingHeavy mechanical cooling most of the year, or capacity loss on the hottest hours. Expect higher power use and a bigger cooling plant.Budget for it explicitly. In dry-hot places (Phoenix type) also check the Water grade, since evaporative cooling uses a lot of water.

A practical way to combine the pieces

  1. Start with the overall grade and the three headline chips (time to power, hazard, policy). These screen sites in or out.
  2. Check timing risk in "What this means for a 75 MW plan". A Moderate or Bad time to power means a later opening and lost revenue: about $48M of 10-year profit per 6 months late in the Prototype's Texas case.
  3. Then read cooling climate for the operating-cost and equipment side. Two sites with the same overall grade can differ a lot here.
  4. Cross-check water when climate is Demanding, and the zero-carbon share of nearby plants if carbon matters to the customer.

Example. Ashburn, VA and Houston, TX can both screen acceptably on the Prototype's grades. Ashburn is Mild and Houston is Demanding. With everything else equal, that tilts the operating-cost and cooling-capex question toward Ashburn, even though neither number moves the overall grade.

5. Limits an investor should know

  • It describes the climate, not a cooling design. It does not say how much power a given chiller plant would use or what PUE you would get.
  • Model grid, not a weather station. ERA5 values are about 10 km cells and can differ from conditions at the site, especially near coasts and mountains.
  • Three years is a short record. It captures recent conditions, not rare extremes or long-term change.
  • Wet-bulb is calculated with Stull's formula (about ±1 °C in the stated range), so humid-hour counts are screening numbers.
  • Thresholds are a team assumption (55% / 300 / 100 and 35% / 1,500 / 1,000), not an industry standard. Change them in one place if your engineers prefer others.
