Section 1

The problem

The story in plain English

A developer who wants to build a data center has to answer two linked questions: where can we build, and how do we power it? Power is not one thing. It arrives in stages, from different sources, at different prices and carbon levels, and a site that looks good on a map can be slow, expensive or blocked.

In the packet's benchmark the grid connection for a 75 MW campus comes in steps: 0 → 25 → 50 → 75 MW at months 18, 24 and 30 ASSUMPTION. Until then the developer must cover the gap with onsite power, contracts and batteries, and the customer is trading off speed to power, cost and environmental performance VERIFIED (Problem Statement; intro page).

Who feels this: developers and their finance teams (capital at risk before power is certain), site-selection and energy advisers (many counties, scattered data), and utilities and communities (large new loads). Why investors care: a wrong early site decision is expensive to undo. This last point is general reasoning, not a sourced statistic.

Section 2

Our solution: Peak Power

What it is, in plain English

Peak Power is a planning and operations simulator that runs entirely in the browser, with no accounts or online services needed for the core product PROTOTYPE. It helps a developer pick a site and a power plan together, then replays every hour of a 10-year plan (87,600 hours) to show cost, carbon and how the plan behaves in a grid emergency.

Peak Power shows a data-center developer where they can build, how to power it, and what every alternative costs in time, money and carbon, before they commit.

Who it is designed for, and the decisions it supports

  • Audience: a data-center developer and the advisers around them ASSUMPTION. The files name the developer as the customer; no real customer has been interviewed UNVERIFIED.
  • Decisions: which project or county looks strongest; when it could open; which power sources to combine and in what size; what it costs; how clean it is; what happens during grid stress; what the cleaner or faster alternative gives up.
  • Hard requirements vs preferences: a hard requirement (for example a carbon limit or a service deadline) removes plans; a preference only ranks the plans that remain. Business revenue is kept apart from power cost. PROTOTYPE

How it works, two ways

For non-technical investors

"Think of it as a flight simulator for a power plant. We feed in every hour of a ten-year period: how much power the campus needs, how much wind and sun there is, and what electricity costs. We test each plan hour by hour. If it can't keep the lights on or meet a requirement, it's thrown out. What's left is ranked by what the customer cares about."

For technical judges

"A library of 384 pre-simulated plans, each replayed through the packet's official checker (all pass). The browser engine re-dispatches chosen capacities over 87,600 hours with rule-based merit-order logic: grid-delivered contracts and merchant imports share the connection limit; onsite resources and a 4-hour battery balance the rest; during stress, Tier 0 is protected, Tier 1 can relocate, Tier 2 can pause. It is not an optimizer."

Section 7

Investor and judge Q&A

Each answer is 20–40 seconds spoken. If you do not know: say "we haven't verified that; here is what we do know," then give the closest verified fact. Never invent a result.

1. What makes Peak Power different?
It joins two things usually done apart: a nine-variable screen of every US county, and an hour-by-hour ten-year simulation of the power plan for a campus, in one offline tool with the assumptions visible. We haven't benchmarked against commercial tools, so we won't claim we are better, only that this combination is what we built.
Evidence: prototype; 384/384 plans pass the packet checker. Competitor comparison: UNVERIFIED
2. Who would pay for this?
We believe developers and the advisers who screen sites for them, because it compresses early screening and shows the trade-offs. But we haven't interviewed customers or tested pricing yet, so that is a hypothesis.
Evidence: none in the materials. UNVERIFIED Pricing ideas are PLANNED proposals only.
3. How reliable is the underlying data?
It is a mix, and we label which is which. Published data: ERCOT 2023, FEMA, WRI water, LBNL queue, CAIDA fiber. Packet assumptions: the fictional campus, prices and stress events. Team assumptions: grading thresholds and the Texas energy screen. Known gaps are shown: 62 counties without water data, 9 without hazard, 35 without a queue region.
Evidence: DATA_GAPS.md; PROTOTYPE_INTEGRATION.md "Data classes". VERIFIED
4. How do you validate site recommendations?
Two levels. The power plans are replayed through the packet's official checker for physical balance and accounting, and all 384 pass. The site grades are not validated against real project outcomes; they are transparent rules over published sources, so a judge can challenge each one.
Evidence: verify_plan_library.py result; "Where these grades come from" list. Real-world validation: UNVERIFIED
5. What happens when grid information is unavailable?
We don't guess. The tool shows "Not graded" or "Data needed". Outside Texas the energy grade is not computed, and the Overall grade uses the grid-queue wait as a stand-in. 102 counties have no queue median at all. A grid-delay input lets you test what a late connection does to a project.
Evidence: DATA_GAPS.md §2; prototype.js (LAYTXT, projDims). VERIFIED
6. Why is Texas better covered than other states?
The benchmark campus and hourly data are ERCOT, so dispatch and the energy screen exist only there. The map itself covers all 3,142 counties. Extending energy needs separate data per region, such as EIA-861 prices and reliability and ISO congestion data; we have not loaded them.
Evidence: DATA_GAPS.md recommended order. PLANNED
7. How does BESS affect the analysis?
Under the V2 rules a 4-hour battery up to 53 MW may charge from any source, and its clean credit is proportional to how clean that charging was, so grid-charged energy earns none. Result: the cheapest plan is battery-free because the battery's capital cost exceeds the savings; the lowest-carbon plan uses a 30 MW battery. The browser plan library only tests 30 MW batteries, and our battery dispatch is rule-based, so storage may be undervalued. We have no real battery project data.
Evidence: summary.json; carbon_alternative.json; DATA_GAPS.md §3. VERIFIED
8. How much does cleaner supply cost?
In the validated packet run, about $116 million more over ten years (+41%) for roughly 95 kg/MWh less carbon, which works out to about $253 per tonne by our arithmetic. Choose it if the customer has a carbon target worth more than that price. On screen, read the live figure.
Evidence: official_check.json vs carbon_alternative.json; $/t is derived. VERIFIED
9. Why do the on-screen numbers differ from your packet outputs?
Two calculation paths. The browser engine maximizes 10-year profit across its 384-plan library; the Python/packet path minimizes cost. They share the packet's rules and both are checker-validated, but they pick different plans. We quote whichever source we are showing, never both together.
Evidence: README.md "Implemented approach"; DEMO_SCRIPT.md. VERIFIED
10. Can the platform scale nationwide?
The site map already does. The power simulation does not: it needs hourly price, wind, solar and load data for each region, and we only have ERCOT. We haven't estimated the effort to extend it, so we won't put a timeline on it.
Evidence: DATA_GAPS.md. Effort estimate: UNVERIFIED
11. What are the technical limitations?
Dispatch is rule-based, not an optimizer, so we show screening comparisons, not proven optima. No startup, ramping, outage or gas-delivery detail. The benchmark repeats one reference year. Time to power uses generation-queue waits as a proxy. And the browser and Python engines are separate.
Evidence: README.md "Limitations"; summary.json limitations. VERIFIED
12. What evidence supports the project's impact?
Honestly, we have no measured real-world impact: no pilot and no users. What we can show is a quantified trade-off on a validated benchmark, in dollars, carbon and timing, and a transparent method. Impact on real decisions is what the next step would test.
Evidence: official checker outputs. Real-world impact: UNVERIFIED
13. How do hard requirements differ from preferences in your tool?
A hard requirement, such as a carbon limit or deadline, removes plans. A preference only ranks what is left. In our demo, a carbon limit below 200 kg/MWh removes the default plan, and the table shows what the limit costs and when to relax it.
Evidence: DEMO_SCRIPT.md (example: $341M to $512M, about $436/t; read live). PROTOTYPE
14. What would be required to commercialize it?
Real market and grid data replacing the fictional benchmark; one unified engine; a commercial weather licence, since the free Open-Meteo tier is non-commercial; hosting and support; and validation against a real developer's site decision.
Evidence: peak-power/README.md (licence); DATA_GAPS.md. PLANNED
15. What is the next major development milestone?
Load the public datasets named in our data-gap report in this order: EIA-861 industrial prices and reliability by state; ISO/RTO congestion for the regions we already name; then utility capacity statements for named projects, each as its own labelled variable. Then test with a developer.
Evidence: DATA_GAPS.md "Recommended order". Not started. PLANNED
When you are uncertain. Use this three-part shape: "We haven't verified that. What we do know is… and here's how we'd find out." Short, calm, no apology. Never fill the silence with a number you can't point to on the screen.
